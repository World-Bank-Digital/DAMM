Egypt — Strategic Foresight

Pre-review draft. Method: scenarios -> preferred future -> backcasting to milestones — the method is declared in the model file and is not yet ratified. Assessment year 2026.

Scenarios

Scenarios bound the uncertainty. They are plausible futures, not forecasts, and none of them is a recommendation.

Rider on the Canal

Digital agriculture in Egypt to 2036 advances mainly as an attachment to water infrastructure, because that is where the money structurally is. Measured: OECD records general services support at 2.6% of the value of agricultural production in 2022-24, of which 76% goes to irrigation and 14% to R&D — leaving roughly a tenth of a very thin envelope for information systems, extension, inspection and market data. Chosen: rather than fight for an incremental GSSE claim, government routes digital spend through irrigation modernisation and through the World Bank-financed Climate Resilient Agri-Food Transformation Project (P180480, US$250m, MWRI/MoE/MALR), whose objective already covers the national agricultural information and early warning system. By 2036 Egypt has dense canal-level telemetry, water-allocation dashboards, and a functioning weather/early-warning backbone — consistent with already-strong A1 (mean 3.6) and C1 (mean 4.0) and with 1.2 and 2.9 at L5. What does not move is the farmer-facing and governance layer: the L1 rows (3.5, 3.11, 4.4, 4.5, 6.9, 6.12) stay at or near L1 because nobody's capital budget owns them. Advisory content, agri-market information and farmer data rights remain by-products of an engineering programme. MWRI becomes the de facto digital agriculture ministry; MALR's Economic Affairs Sector keeps its published digitalization vision but not the budget line to execute it. Egypt looks strong on infrastructure indices and weak on service uptake — a plausible outcome, not a desirable one.

Driven by: Structural dominance of irrigation in the GSSE envelope (measured: 76% of a 2.6%-of-production-value envelope), Availability of a large climate-finance vehicle already covering information and early-warning systems (P180480), Water scarcity and Nile allocation pressure as the top-order political problem, Fragmented mandate (MALR / MoSIT / MWRI) resolved de facto by whoever holds capital, not by statute, Existing A1/C1 strength making infrastructure additions technically easy to absorb

What would bring it about: A choice — explicit or by default — to treat digital agriculture as an eligible cost inside irrigation and climate-resilience investment rather than as a standalone programme; continued absence of a ring-fenced budget code for agricultural information services; MWRI retaining implementation leadership on P180480-type operations; and no binding requirement that farmer-facing service levels be reported alongside infrastructure outputs.

Implication: Sector capability becomes lopsided and hard to redirect: pillar A1 and C1 continue to advance while E1 (2.17), C4 (2.33) and C2 (2.25) stagnate, and the roadmap's leverage shrinks to negotiating digital riders on engineering contracts. Any later attempt to build advisory, market-information or data-rights functions starts from L1 with no institutional owner, and the withheld indicators in C2 and E1 stay unobservable because no programme generates the reporting that would populate them.

The Card Becomes the Spine

Egypt drives digitalisation from administrative identity outward. Measured: the agricultural holding system (Farmer's Card) already exists and the FY2025/26 development plan targets 80% coverage of holdings, with the Ministry of Planning as plan owner and MALR/MWRI implementing; contract farming is targeted at 1.8 million feddans; Law No. 151 of 2020 provides a horizontal personal-data baseline. Chosen: government treats the registry as the upstream dependency for everything, and ties subsidy eligibility, input allocation, contract-farming enrolment and eventually credit to card status. By 2036 the registry is near-universal and interoperable with national ID and payment rails; C2 and C4 rise sharply from their 2026 emerging means because the state now measures, and needs, holding-level data. The 160,000 STAR households in Minya, Assiut and Sohag are onboarded as the first at-scale cohort, giving Upper Egypt an unusually complete data picture. The binding uncertainty is governance, not coverage. If Law 151's executive regulations, licensing and redress machinery are actually operationalised for farmer data, Egypt gets a rights-respecting public data spine. If not, the card becomes a compliance instrument: farmers enrol because exclusion is costly, consent is nominal, and the L1 rows on data rights and grievance (4.4, 4.5, 6.9, 6.12) stay L1 while registry coverage indicators hit L4-L5. That divergence — high capability, thin legitimacy — is the scenario's defining risk, and it is a choice, not a forecast.

Driven by: An existing, politically owned registry target (80% of holdings) with a named plan owner, Fiscal pressure to make price and input support targetable, given total support at 2.8% of GDP, Contract-farming expansion requiring verifiable holding identity, Law 151/2020 and the pace of its executive regulations as the swing variable on legitimacy, Ready-made onboarding cohorts (STAR's 160,000 households; GIZ AIP farmer organisations)

What would bring it about: Sustained inter-ministerial commitment to registry build-out beyond a single plan year; an explicit coordination instrument assigning legal authority across MALR, MoSIT and MWRI rather than assuming a single lead; conditioning subsidy or contract access on registration; and — for the benign branch — MCIT and MALR jointly issuing farmer-data consent, licensing and redress rules under Law 151 before, not after, mass enrolment.

Implication: The registry becomes the sector's chokepoint and its main asset. Sequencing logic becomes unavoidable: nothing downstream (advisory targeting, subsidy reform, insurance, credit scoring) can outrun card coverage and data quality. Roadmap priorities shift toward data governance capability — currently the weakest observed area — because the state's own exposure grows with every service attached to the card. Private and donor systems either integrate with the spine or become irrelevant.

Two Speeds, One Country

Nothing consolidates; the sector splits by market. Measured: institutional mandate is fragmented across MALR, MoSIT and MWRI, which OECD identifies as a source of coordination and implementation failure; O1 sits at 2.5 with only 2 of 7 rows rated; E1 at 2.17 with 3 rows withheld; 4 of 8 C2 rows withheld. Chosen (or defaulted into): no inter-ministerial instrument is created, and digital capability is built by whoever has an external reason to build it. Export horticulture — citrus, table grapes, potatoes, aromatics — invests heavily because EU and Gulf buyers demand traceability, residue records and plot-level provenance. Exporters, packhouses and their contracted growers get lot-level traceability, satellite monitoring and agronomic advisory at commercial quality by 2030. In parallel, donor projects each build their own vertical: STAR runs ICT4D through its Upper Egypt cohort with an IFAD Global Technical Specialist; P180480 builds its own advisory and climate-data layer; the GIZ AIP / IFPRI randomized trial across ~30 farmer organisations and ~4,000 farmers in Minya and Beni Suef generates good evidence for a small population. By 2036 Egypt has excellent digital agriculture for perhaps a tenth of its holdings and thin, project-dependent coverage for the rest. Aggregate pillar means may drift upward while the distribution worsens; the withheld indicators stay withheld because no actor owns national reporting.

Driven by: Export market compliance requirements as the strongest non-state investment signal, Unresolved fragmentation of agricultural policy authority (measured, OECD), Donor project cycles as the de facto planning unit; each with its own results chain and data stack, Very thin general-services envelope preventing any public platform from crowding in the private layer, Weak observed monitoring capability (O1 2.5, 2 of 7 rated) leaving the two speeds unmeasured

What would bring it about: Continued absence of a legally mandated inter-ministerial coordination body; tightening of buyer traceability rules in EU and Gulf markets; donor portfolios renewed as successor projects rather than folded into a government programme; and no decision to make the Farmer's Card or a national data exchange a mandatory integration point for private and project systems.

Implication: Capability exists but is not national, not interoperable and not inheritable — when projects close, their stacks decay. The policy question becomes salvage and interoperability rather than construction: whether the state can require the export layer and the project layer to write into common registries and standards. Smallholder-facing rows (3.5, 3.11, 5.5, 5.7, 6.1) remain the weakest, and Egypt's assessment scores become progressively less informative as means average across two structurally different sectors.

The preferred future

A normative selection — a claim about values, not a finding from evidence. It is proposed for decision, not asserted.

The Rights-Bearing Spine and the Serving Canal

Egypt's preferred future is not a fourth scenario; it is the deliberate combination of the benign branch of one scenario with the assets of another, plus an explicit refusal of the third. Measured (evidence, not preference): general services support stands at 2.6% of the value of agricultural production in 2022-24, of which 76% goes to irrigation and 14% to R&D; total support is about 2.8% of GDP; the Farmer's Card holding system exists with an FY2025/26 plan target of 80% coverage of holdings under the Ministry of Planning; contract farming is targeted at 1.8 million feddans; Law No. 151 of 2020 provides a horizontal personal-data baseline; P180480 carries US$250m with a national agricultural information and early-warning objective; STAR covers roughly 160,000 households in Minya, Assiut and Sohag; assessed capability is strong on A1 (3.6) and C1 (4.0), emerging on C2 (2.25), C4 (2.33) and E1 (2.17), and at L1 on the farmer-facing and governance rows (3.5, 3.11, 4.4, 4.5, 6.9, 6.12). Chosen (normative): by 2036 Egypt wants the registry to be the sector's spine and wants that spine to be rights-bearing before it is near-universal. Concretely, the preference is that MCIT and MALR issue farmer-data consent, purpose-limitation, licensing and redress rules under Law 151 ahead of mass enrolment, so that C2 and C4 climb toward L4 while 4.4, 4.5, 6.9 and 6.12 climb with them rather than lagging by a decade. The country would choose registry legitimacy over registry speed where the two conflict — accepting a slower path to 80% coverage if that is the price of consent that is real and grievance channels that function. This is a values claim: nothing in the current evidence base predicts it, and the observed default is enrolment driven by the cost of exclusion. Second, Egypt would choose to keep the canal money but change what it buys. Rather than abandoning the irrigation-financed route that Rider on the Canal describes as structurally rational, the preferred future keeps water infrastructure as the funding vehicle while attaching a binding condition: farmer-facing service levels — advisory reach, agri-market information availability, extension response times — are reported alongside telemetry and hydraulic outputs, and a ring-fenced budget code for agricultural information services is created so that E1, C4 and the L1 advisory rows acquire an institutional owner rather than remaining engineering by-products. Third, Egypt would choose convergence over stratification. The preferred future explicitly rejects the Two Speeds outcome in which export-oriented and reclamation-zone agriculture reaches L4-L5 service quality while Upper Egypt smallholders and tenants stay at L1. The chosen instrument is sequencing: the STAR cohort in Minya, Assiut and Sohag and the GIZ AIP farmer organisations are treated as the first at-scale onboarding population, not the last, so that the most complete holding-level data picture in the country is built in its poorest governorates. What this future does not claim: that it is likely, that financing exists for it, or that inter-ministerial authority is currently constituted to deliver it. Those are open questions the roadmap must answer.

What is being chosen: This is a normative selection — a statement of values and priorities, not a finding from evidence, and not a forecast. Three things are being chosen. (1) Sequencing legitimacy ahead of coverage: farmer-data consent, licensing and redress machinery under Law 151/2020 is operationalised before the registry approaches its 80%-of-holdings target, accepting slower enrolment as the price. (2) Redirecting, not replacing, the irrigation-dominated funding envelope: digital agriculture continues to ride water and climate finance (P180480 and successors), but on condition that farmer-facing service levels are reported alongside infrastructure outputs and that a ring-fenced budget code gives agricultural information services an institutional owner. (3) Convergence over stratification: Upper Egypt smallholder cohorts (the ~160,000 STAR households; GIZ AIP farmer organisations) are onboarded first rather than last, deliberately foregoing the faster returns available from export and reclamation-zone farms. Each of these is a trade-off against a cheaper or faster alternative that current incentives favour. The measured baselines (GSSE composition, capability means, legal instruments in force) are evidence; the ordering of priorities among them is a choice.

Who would have to agree: Cabinet-level agreement is the binding requirement, because no single ministry can deliver this. Specifically: the Ministry of Planning and Economic Development, as owner of the development plan that carries the 80% holdings target, would have to accept a legitimacy-conditioned enrolment schedule rather than a coverage-maximising one. MALR (Economic Affairs Sector) and MWRI would have to accept a formal coordination instrument assigning legal authority rather than letting capital budgets decide leadership by default. MCIT and the data-protection authority constituted under Law 151/2020 would have to issue farmer-specific consent, licensing and redress rules, and accept enforcement responsibility in a sector where enrolment is subsidy-linked. The Ministry of Finance would have to create and defend a ring-fenced budget code for agricultural information services against the structural pull of irrigation capital. Financiers — the World Bank on P180480-type operations, IFAD on STAR, GIZ on AIP — would have to accept farmer-facing service-level reporting as a covenant, not a soft indicator. Governorate agricultural directorates in Minya, Assiut and Sohag, and the farmer organisations and water user associations themselves, would have to accept being the first at-scale cohort, which means bearing the implementation risk of an untested system. Absent any one of these, the preferred future degrades predictably into one of the three scenarios it was assembled from.
